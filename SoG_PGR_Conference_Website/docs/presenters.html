Rayan Onyonka, ISDS

Title: Large language models for localised climate solutions: What can AI language models tell us about how textual information influences understanding of environmental issues?

Abstract: Place-based climate action has emerged as a key method for the delivery of effective and context-specific responses to challenges at the local and regional levels. However, meaningful action depends heavily on access to reliable, expert knowledge presented in a format that is understandable and actionable for non-specialists, grounded in the local context.

Large Language Models (LLMs) show promise as a method to deliver complex climate information in an accessible format, however, their effectiveness in local climate contexts is limited by possible hallucination and a lack of access to place-specific information. They have also not been tested yet in real-word context to evaluate their effectiveness in assisting local climate action. To address this, we propose a retrieval augmented (RAG) LLM assistant that integrates various local and national documents and policies with scientific reports to provide climate information that is grounded in local context.

After technical evaluation, the assistant was tested in the context of a mini‑public on local climate action in Kirklees. Participants were asked to use the assistant as a source of information during the session to inform the creation of local climate‑related proposals, which were then forwarded to the local council for assessment. This process and the resulting proposals were compared against those produced by a control group operating without access to the assistant. Initial analysis suggests that retrieval‑based grounding improves the local relevance and interpretability of generated outputs and supports the development of more actionable proposals suitable for implementation by community groups or local councils.

Lightning Talks:

Reece Sawyer, RBPM

Title: A remote sensing approach to glacier darkening in Europe

Abstract: Glacier surface darkening can enhance melt by reducing albedo and increasing absorption of shortwave radiation, but the relative influence of changing surface state, atmospheric deposition and biological darkening remains difficult to distinguish at regional scales. This study investigates spatial and temporal glacier darkening across the European Alps using satellite remote sensing. As an initial benchmark, MODIS summer albedo was analysed across Switzerland from 2015 - 2024 using a fixed early-period glacier reference footprint. Annual June-August observations were reduced to median composites and pixel-wise albedo trends calculated. MODIS cells showed a mean trend of -0.00338 yr⁻¹, with 76.99% exhibiting darkening. Strong interannual variability was observed, including an exceptionally low-albedo summer in 2022, while the strongest supported negative trends occurred within the central glacier elevation range. These results demonstrate widespread but spatially and temporally variable darkening across the Swiss glacier reference domain. Ongoing work uses higher-resolution Sentinel-2 imagery to resolve seasonal surface-state evolution and localised darkening, before integrating atmospheric reanalysis and spectral indicators to investigate atmospheric and biologically driven contributions.

Kelly O’Shea, RBPM

Title: Determining Environmental Impact - Why Soil Matters

Abstract: Soil is often treated as a waste sink, reflected in the language we use to describe it: dirty, filthy, or simply “dirt”. Yet soil is a complex, living ecosystem that can take hundreds to thousands of years to form. Beneath our feet, intricate biological, chemical and physical processes underpin the health of terrestrial ecosystems and, ultimately, our own.

Soil microbial communities drive essential ecosystem functions, including nutrient cycling, carbon storage, decomposition and water regulation. They also interact closely with plants, forming symbiotic relationships that support growth, nutrient acquisition, and resistance to pests and disease. Because these processes are largely invisible, people often overlook their importance.

Healthy soils provide essential ecosystem services while also indicating environmental change, responding to pollution, contamination and climate pressures. Conversely, intensive agriculture, mechanisation and the introduction of contaminants such as microplastics and PFAS can disrupt soil communities and alter the movement and persistence of pollutants through ecosystems and food webs.

Understanding soil ecology is therefore not simply about protecting what lies beneath our feet. It is about recognising the connections between soil, plants, animals, environmental health and human wellbeing, and exploring how soil microbiology can help us build healthier, more resilient ecosystems and communities.

Ode Zulkarnain Sahji Tihurua, SJCC

Title: When is a Plantation a Ruin? Colonial Afterlives and the Uneven Postcolonial Landscape

Abstract: When does a colonial plantation become a ruin? This question prompts a consideration of dominant understandings of colonial ruins as discrete, bounded remnants of the colonial past. Drawing on preliminary ethnographic work conducted in August 2026 in the Banda Islands, Indonesia, this article traces how the material, ecological, social, and institutional components that once constituted the colonial nutmeg plantation system followed divergent trajectories after the end of colonial rule. I develop the concept of ‘differential afterlives’ to describe how the heterogeneous components of a plantation formation persist, decay, adapt, or disappear according to distinct yet interconnected temporal trajectories. The varied conditions found across different sites demonstrate that colonial plantations did not become ruins simultaneously or as a coherent whole. The buildings, groves, nutmeg trees, production spaces, labour practices, social relations, and institutions that once formed the plantation persisted, decayed, grew, were repurposed, or were reconfigured through distinct temporalities. By shifting the focus from ruins as material objects to the differential processes through which plantation relations persist and transform, the article argues that ruination must be understood as a relational and uneven process. From this perspective, plantation ruins are not merely material evidence of a bygone colonial era but active components of the processes that continuously shape the postcolonial landscape of the Banda Islands.

Yue Che, SJCC

Title: Rethinking the role of state-owned enterprises in Chinese urban governance: Taking the SOE jiashuyuan regeneration as an example

Abstract: Research on political-business relations in China has largely focused on the collusion between stateowned enterprises (SOEs) and local governments. However, with the growth of the private sector and ongoing urbanization, the roles of SOEs, the private sector, and local states require further exploration. This research examines the urban renewal process of a residential community built by an SOE in a small industrial city in western China, exploring the roles of state-business relations in this process. Through ethnographic fieldwork, this research reveals that SOE collaborates closely with local governments and developers, using its influence to build a connection between local states and residents, reduce conflict to ensure project progress, and use collaborative frameworks to seek their benefits. the research highlights how SOEs play a role in urban renewal, while power struggles between residents and local states impact the SOEs’ ability to gain further resources and influence, to contribute new theoretical insights into the role of state-business relations in China’s urbanization.

Prahelika Deka, SJCC

Title: (Re)Imagining climate futures: An ethnographic and creative exploration of futuremaking in Majuli Island, India

Abstract: This project investigates the alternative, possible imagined futures of Majuli Island in Assam, India, based on people’s social and temporal imaginaries. In the face of a rapidly changing climate and increasing reporting of floods and erosion, popular narratives of the island depict a dystopian future of complete submergence of the island. While dominant climate narratives portray Majuli’s doomed future, this thesis argues that residents already inhabit multiple, competing futures that challenge this singular dystopian imaginary. Within such a scenario, this study examines how people imagine their futures as well as their island/river’s futures and how intersecting social identities such as spiritual and everyday beliefs, age and gender influence their imagination.

Using participant-led, creative methodologies, I respond to the emerging notion of an imagination crisis in climate change research. In doing so, I also engage with emerging literature on imagination, futures and plurality of waters and examine the everyday ways of life, the relationships people share with their land/river and the emotions that drive the meaning-making of both space and time. I argue there is not a lack of imagination but the failure in identifying and/or accommodating multiple alternate imagined futures from dominant climate imaginaries. This research is therefore significant and timely in addressing the frontline climate survivors’ visions in Majuli and similar areas in the public and non-public discourses.
